ABSTRACT (<= 600 words; structured as Background / Objectives / Methods / Results
/ Conclusions, following the sample)
  - Background: MCI is a decision point, not a diagnosis - some patients convert
    to AD, most do not, and the tests that separate them differ wildly in cost.
  - Objectives: quantify what each additional modality buys, in accuracy AND in
    confidence, using models that are sparse and interpretable by construction.
  - Methods: CLG-Lasso (Laplace-prior logistic regression, cyclic coordinate
    descent) implemented from scratch; 776-patient tri-modal ADNI cohort at t=0;
    five models from three modalities; every model reports a confidence x
    correctness breakdown; results resampled over 20 stratified splits.
  - Results: tri-modal fusion reaches ROC-AUC 0.808 +/- 0.020 resampled
    (0.827 [0.766, 0.881] on the canonical split) on just 7 of 369 features;
    accumulating modalities strictly beats swapping them; 36-40 % of patients
    cannot be confidently called by any model.
  - Conclusions: the headline number is not the finding - the finding is that a
    new modality buys confidence on a small minority and a net accuracy gain far
    smaller than the churn it causes.

CHAPTER 1 - INTRODUCTION                                    (~1,100 words)
===============================================================================

1.1 Background and Motivation
  - Alzheimer's disease and the MCI prodrome: why the conversion question is the
    clinically actionable one.
  - The three modalities and their real-world cost gradient: MMSE (bedside,
    minutes, free) -> MRI (scan, expensive, scheduled) -> CSF (lumbar puncture,
    invasive, refused by some patients).
  - Why point accuracy is the wrong single number in a clinical setting: a model
    that is confidently wrong is worse than one that abstains.

1.2 Problem Statement
  - Given only baseline (t=0) measurements, predict conversion - and say how
    sure the model is.
  - Two questions the project actually asks: (i) what does each modality add,
    in accuracy and in confidence? (ii) when a cheap model is unsure, does a
    more expensive one actually resolve those specific patients?

1.3 Aims and Objectives
  - Implement a sparse Bayesian logistic classifier (CLG-Lasso) from first
    principles rather than calling a library.
  - Build one leak-free tri-modal cohort and one canonical split so every model
    is comparable on identical held-out patients.
  - Report confidence x correctness for every model, not just AUC.
  - Compare two ways of adding a modality: swapping it in, or accumulating it.

1.4 Contributions
  - A from-scratch CLG-Lasso solver with per-model feature-selection exports.
  - A reproducible, leakage-audited ADNI baseline pipeline: one fitting path for
    all five models, absolute paths, one canonical split read by RID.
  - Empirical answer to "swap vs. accumulate": accumulation is strictly better,
    and the reason is visible in the fitted weights.
  - A quantified confidence ceiling: 36-40 % of MCI patients cannot be called
    confidently by any combination of these three baseline modalities, and on
    that group single-modality models score below the majority baseline.

1.5 Dissertation Overview
  - One paragraph per chapter.

    deployed triage policy.

2.8 Research Gap

    costs, who it changes its mind about, or how many patients it is

CHAPTER 5 - SINGLE-MODALITY AND FUSION MODELS               (~2,600 words)
===============================================================================

5.1 Introduction
  - The question: what does each modality buy?
  - How results are reported in this chapter, stated once and applied
    throughout: every headline AUC is the mean +/- sd over the 20 resampled
    stratified 70/30 splits of stage 09, with lambda re-selected inside every
    training set and preprocessing re-fitted per split. The single canonical
    split is reported alongside, with its bootstrap CI, as the fit whose
    weights and feature lists are inspected later in the chapter.
  - Why this way round: a single 70/30 split is itself a draw, and on 233 test
    patients the difference between draws is not small. Leading with the
    resampled number and treating the single split as one observation of it
    avoids quoting a figure the data does not support.
  - The two intervals answer different questions and are never combined: sd is
    the spread across splits (which patients land in test), the bootstrap CI is
    sampling of test patients within one split. Cross-reference 4.7.
  - All five models are fitted by one stage on one split, so the comparison is
    like-for-like by construction, not by convention.

5.2 Single-Modality Models
  - MMSE: 0.664 +/- 0.031 (single split 0.682 [0.606, 0.753]), 9 of 37 features
    kept, lambda=16 - the bedside floor, and the only model that never leaves it.
  - MRI: 0.747 +/- 0.029 (single split 0.784 [0.720, 0.850]), 36 of 323 kept,
    lambda=12 - the largest feature space and the heaviest cost, at scan price.
  - CSF: 0.783 +/- 0.028 (single split 0.772 [0.705, 0.836]), 5 of 9 kept,
    lambda=2 - the best single modality under resampling, and the most
    information per feature measured, at lumbar-puncture price.
  - The sensitivity/specificity asymmetry common to all three: sensitivity
    0.24-0.52, specificity 0.84-0.91. Every model is far better at ruling out
    conversion than ruling it in. Say this here, it recurs in every later table.
    MMSE is the extreme case - sensitivity 0.202 against specificity 0.966 on
    the canonical split, i.e. it barely identifies converters at all.

5.3 Fusion Models
  - MMSE+MRI: 0.758 +/- 0.027 (single split 0.797 [0.729, 0.863]), 46 of 360
    non-zero, lambda=12.
  - MMSE+MRI+CSF: 0.808 +/- 0.020 (single split 0.827 [0.766, 0.881]), only
    7 of 369 non-zero at lambda=48 - the headline model, and the sparsity is the
    interesting part.
  - The tri-modal model also has the SMALLEST spread across splits (sd 0.020 vs
    0.027-0.031 for the rest): more modalities buy stability, not just level.
  - The tension on the single split: highest AUC but lower balanced accuracy
    (0.628) and sensitivity (0.309) - discuss threshold effects rather than
    pretending it is a clean win. Under resampling the tension mostly
    disappears (bal. acc 0.678, the best of the five), which is itself evidence
    that the 0.628 was a property of that one split.
  - Adding MRI to MMSE does NOT reach CSF alone (0.758 vs 0.783). The gain in
    the tri-modal model is carried by CSF, not by fusion as such.

5.4 The Hierarchy
  - Resampled ordering: MMSE+MRI+CSF 0.808 > CSF 0.783 > MMSE+MRI 0.758 >
    MRI 0.747 > MMSE 0.664.
  - This is NOT the single-split ordering, which put MRI (0.784) above CSF
    (0.772) and MMSE+MRI (0.797) above both. CSF moves from fourth to second.
    The reordering is the chapter's argument for reporting resampled numbers,
    and should be shown as a paired figure rather than asserted.
  - What the data separates and what it does not: single-split CIs are ~0.13
    wide on 233 patients, and only the tri-modal interval clears the MMSE
    model's. Adjacent models in the ranking above are not distinguished.
  - What survives both views: MMSE is the floor, the tri-modal model is the
    ceiling, and everything between them is one undifferentiated band.
  - lambda stability as a diagnostic: a lambda that jumps between resamples
    means the regularisation strength is weakly identified by this much data;
    report the per-split lambda alongside the per-split AUC.
  - Figures: cv_auc_distribution.png; paired single-split vs. resampled ranking.
  - Cost overlay: the ranking is not a recommendation until price is attached -
    CSF buys second place at LP cost, MMSE holds last place for free. This sets
    up Chapter 6.

5.5 Which Features Survive
  - Retention RATE tracks how informative a modality is, per feature: CSF keeps
    5 of 9 (55.6 %) and ranks second; MRI keeps 36 of 323 (11.1 %) and ranks
    fourth. A CSF analyte carries far more of the signal than a DK region does.
  - Absolute COUNT does not: the tri-modal model is the strongest and keeps the
    fewest, 7 of 369 (98.1 % sparse). Do not overstate this into "informative
    models keep more features" - the data contradicts it, and the honest version
    is the more interesting claim.
  - The strongest single observation in the chapter: the tri-modal model keeps
    ZERO of the 37 MMSE features - its 7 survivors are 2 CSF (TAU/ABETA42 ratio,
    ABETA42) and 5 MRI (ST29SV, ST12SV, ST83TA, ST111TA, ST99TA). Given CSF and
    MRI, the cognitive score adds nothing the lasso will pay for. Compare with
    MMSE+MRI, which keeps 8 MMSE features when CSF is absent - MMSE is carrying
    a signal that CSF carries better.
  - The explanation to argue: with all three modalities available the lambda
    search can run to 48 because a handful of cross-modal features reproduce
    what dozens of within-modality features were each partly carrying. Fusion
    buys sparsity as well as accuracy.
  - Which specific features recur: the DK regions and MMSE items that survive
    across resamples, and how often each is selected (selection-rate table).
  - Stability caveat: feature counts and weights quoted here are from the
    canonical single-split fit; which features survive varies across resamples,
    so report selection frequency, not a single kept-set.
  - The nesting caveat restated: MMSE items are exactly nested inside MMSCORE,
    so the lasso keeps whichever collinear copy it reaches first - weights rank
    features, they do not size effects.

5.6 Confidence x Correctness Across Models
  - Confidence matrices side by side; confidently-wrong counts as the
    comparison that matters clinically.
  - The tri-modal model is confident (>= 0.80) about only 21 of 233 held-out
    patients - 9 % - but is right on 19 of those 21. High precision, tiny
    coverage: the model that scores best is also the one that answers least.
  - Tie back to 5.2: high specificity with low sensitivity means the confident
    quadrant is dominated by non-converters (only 1 of the 21 is a converter).
    State who the confidently-wrong patients are, not just how many.
  - Whether the AUC hierarchy of 5.4 is the same hierarchy under
    confidently-wrong count - if it is not, that is a finding.
  - Source: stage 11 confidence-band workbooks, which carry every patient's
    band for both fusion models with train rows scored out-of-fold.

CHAPTER 6 - THE MODALITY HANDOFF: SWAPPING VS. ACCUMULATING (~2,400 words)
===============================================================================

6.1 Introduction
  - A narrower question on its own footing: take the patients the cheap model
    was NOT confident about, and ask what the next model does with those exact
    patients - does it become confident, and does the answer change?
  - This is where the cost argument of the dissertation now lives: not as a
    deployed triage policy, but as a direct measurement of what the expensive
    test resolves.

6.2 Experimental Setup
  - Fresh stratified 50/50 split (388 train / 388 test, seed 42), independent of
    the canonical split; numbers here are lower by construction and are NOT
    comparable to Chapter 5. State this before any table.
  - Every one of the 776 gets a probability from every model (test rows from the
    fitted model, train rows out-of-fold) so confident populations can be
    counted directly.

6.3 Who Is Each Classifier Confident About?
  - Confidence counts at 0.70 and 0.80 with accuracy and confidently-wrong
    inside each band.
  - MMSE is the outlier: max confidence 0.741, essentially never clears 0.80,
    and is right only 74 % of the time where it is confident at 0.70 (vs. MRI
    83 %, CSF 87 %). This is why the source gate is 0.70.

6.4 Chain A - Swapping Modalities (stage 13): MMSE -> MRI -> CSF
  6.4.1 Hop 1, MMSE -> MRI: 224 handed off (the harder half, 43.8 % converters);
        46 become confident (34 right / 12 confidently wrong); 37 fixed, 30
        broken, net +7.
  6.4.2 Hop 2, MRI -> CSF: 178 handed off; 24 become confident (21/3); 32 fixed,
        26 broken, net +6. Mean confidence actually FALLS by 0.008.
  6.4.3 The churn is the point - a 3-point accuracy gain is 67 patients changing
        sides, not 7 patients improving.
  6.4.4 End to end: 154 of 388 (40 %) that no single modality can call, and on
        those every model scores BELOW the majority baseline - the gate is
        correctly identifying patients on whom all three are worse than guessing.

6.5 Chain B - Accumulating Modalities (stage 14): MMSE -> MMSE+MRI -> +CSF
  6.5.1 Identical machinery, identical split, each step ADDS a modality.
  6.5.2 Hop 1: 51 become confident (37/14); 34 fixed, 22 broken, net +12.
  6.5.3 Hop 2: 31 become confident (29/2 - only 2 confidently wrong); 21 fixed,
        5 broken, net +16; accuracy 0.647 -> 0.740 on those 173.
  6.5.4 End to end: 142 of 388 (36.6 %) unclaimed vs. 154 for swapping, and the
        tri-modal model reaches 0.697 on those 142 - above the majority baseline,
        where every swapping model fell below it.

6.6 Why Accumulating Wins
  - The fusion model cannot lose what MMSE and MRI already knew.
  - Its lambda search runs over 369 features rather than 9, so it can keep a CSF
    signal AND the imaging evidence that contradicts it.
  - Evidence from the fitted weights, not assertion - and note the connection to
    5.5, where the tri-modal model's 7 survivors are exactly a CSF core plus MRI
    corroboration.

6.7 Gate Sensitivity
  - The sweep sheets (gates 0.55 -> 0.90) make the gate choice visible rather
    than assumed; show that the conclusion is not an artefact of 0.70/0.80.

6.8 Discussion
  - The consistent finding across both chains: a new modality buys confidence on
    a small minority and a net accuracy gain far smaller than the churn it
    causes - roughly one patient broken for every 1.2 fixed when swapping.

CHAPTER 7 - DISCUSSION                                      (~1,500 words)

7.1 Synthesis - answering the two research questions directly.

7.2 What Each Modality Actually Buys
  - Cost-per-AUC-point framing across MMSE / MRI / CSF.
  - The 36-40 % of patients nobody can call, and what that implies about a
    baseline-only prediction ceiling.
  - The MMSE result stated plainly: given CSF and MRI, the tri-modal lasso keeps
    none of it. Cheap does not mean complementary.

7.3 Sparsity as a Clinical Asset
  - A 7-feature tri-modal model is auditable; a 369-feature one is not.
  - Caveat about instability of which features are kept across splits.

7.4 Confidence as a Deployment Contract
  - A model that abstains on 40 % and is right 90 % of the rest may be more
    useful than one that answers everything at 76 %.
  - How a clinic would actually operate this, and the honest limit: at the 0.80
    band the tri-modal model answers only 9 % of patients.

7.5 Limitations
  - Single cohort (ADNI), no external validation - the headline risk.
  - Baseline-only by design; no longitudinal signal used.
  - Conversion label depends on follow-up length; some "stable" patients are
    under-observed.
  - CIs ~0.13 wide - most model comparisons are not resolved by 233 patients.
  - Confidence here is a predicted-probability band, not a calibrated posterior;
    no calibration curve / Brier decomposition performed.
  - Costs are argued qualitatively, not with a formal health-economic model, and
    no deployed triage policy is evaluated - Chapter 6 measures the information
    a second test adds, not the policy that would act on it.
  - Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 use different splits and are not comparable.
  - Boundary-distance analysis (stage 12) excluded from this dissertation.
